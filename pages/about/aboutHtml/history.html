THE LAND BEFORE KINGDOMS
PREHISTORY · AROUND 14,400 YEARS AGO

A Bread-Making Discovery That Changed History

In the Black Desert of northeastern Jordan, archaeologists uncovered charred remains of bread-like food at the Natufian site of Shubayqa 1 . The fragments date back approximately 14,400 years and represent the earliest direct archaeological evidence of bread-making reported by the researchers.

These small remains reveal something extraordinary: hunter-gatherers were preparing flatbread-like food from wild cereals and other plants long before farming became widespread. The research showed that bread-making preceded the development of agriculture in the region by at least 4,000 years.

The discovery, published in 2018 in the scientific journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences , gives Jordan an important place in the study of food, innovation and early human societies. Scientists did not find a complete modern-looking loaf, but charred food fragments examined under the microscope.

NEOLITHIC JORDAN

Ain Ghazal and the First Settled Communities

Thousands of years before the appearance of modern cities, communities in the area of present-day Amman established settlements, cultivated plants and experimented with new ways of living. Ain Ghazal is among the best-known sites associated with this transformation from mobile groups to more settled village life.

Its remarkable plaster statues, dating back roughly 9,700 years, are among the earliest large-scale representations of the human form. Their careful construction suggests complex social practices, artistic skill and symbolic traditions that still fascinate archaeologists.

Visitors can explore this chapter of human history through the collections of The Jordan Museum in Amman, which presents artefacts and stories covering an immense span of the region's past.

ANCIENT CIVILIZATIONS
IRON AGE · FIRST MILLENNIUM BCE

Ammon, Moab and Edom

Long before the modern borders of Jordan existed, the central and southern highlands were home to distinct Iron Age kingdoms. Ammon was associated with the area around present-day Amman, Moab occupied much of the central plateau east of the Dead Sea, and Edom developed farther south.

These kingdoms participated in regional trade, agriculture and diplomacy while maintaining their own rulers and local traditions. Fortified settlements, inscriptions, pottery and religious objects provide evidence of everyday life and political change. The region was never isolated: it formed part of a wider network connected to the Levant, Arabia and Mesopotamia.

Ancient Rabbath-Ammon, now part of Amman's historical landscape, is an accessible starting point for learning about the Ammonite past. The surviving remains also remind us that buildings visible today may belong to much later periods than the earliest inhabitants.

NABATAEAN CIVILIZATION

Petra: A City Carved Into History

The Nabataeans built one of the ancient world's most impressive trading centres at Petra . Its position between Arabia, Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean helped merchants connect valuable goods, including incense, spices and textiles, with distant markets.

Yet Petra was much more than a commercial stop. Its carved monuments, tombs, temples and carefully engineered channels and water reservoirs show extraordinary architectural skill and an ability to thrive in a dry mountain environment. The Siq provides a dramatic approach to Al-Khazneh, while other monuments reveal the city's wider cultural and religious life.

In 106 CE the Nabataean kingdom was incorporated into the Roman Empire. Petra continued to experience changes under Roman and Byzantine administration. Today it is recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and remains central to Jordan's cultural identity and tourism.

ROMAN & BYZANTINE ERAS

Cities, Colonnades and Mosaics

Under Roman rule, urban centres in the region grew within a network of roads and trade routes. Jerash , ancient Gerasa, preserves columns, theatres, plazas and temples that help visitors imagine public life during the Roman period. Umm Qais , ancient Gadara, offers another window into the cities of the wider Decapolis region.

In the Byzantine period, Christian communities built churches and decorated sacred spaces with intricate mosaic floors. Umm ar-Rasas , originally associated with a Roman military site, contains churches and mosaics representing cities and landscapes of the region.

Together these archaeological sites demonstrate that the history of Jordan is not a single uninterrupted culture but a succession of communities, religions, artistic styles and institutions leaving visible traces across the country.

UNESCO – Umm ar-Rasas and its mosaics ↗
7TH–8TH CENTURIES AND BEYOND

Early Islamic Jordan and the Desert Castles

In the seventh century, the rise of the early Islamic caliphates reshaped political, economic and cultural connections across the Levant. Jordan became part of an extensive region linking important cities, routes and communities under changing Muslim administrations.

The Umayyad period left a particularly distinctive architectural heritage in the desert east of Amman. Quseir Amra , an early eighth-century desert complex, is famous for its reception hall, bathhouse and exceptional wall paintings. These frescoes preserve scenes of people, animals and celestial imagery that open an unusual window into early Islamic art.

Other desert buildings such as Qasr Kharana and Qasr al-Mushatta show the variety of structures associated with the region. They should not all be treated as military castles: their purposes included residence, administration and hospitality, and some functions remain debated.

UNESCO – Quseir Amra ↗
12TH–16TH CENTURIES

Fortresses and Changing Powers

During the medieval centuries, Jordan's location between Syria, Egypt and the Arabian Peninsula gave its valleys and highlands strategic importance. Crusader forces built or held strongholds along important routes; Karak Castle became a prominent fortress during the twelfth century.

Muslim rulers, including the Ayyubids and later the Mamluks, contested and reorganised this landscape. Ajloun Castle , built under Ayyubid authority in the late twelfth century, helped defend routes and settlements in northern Jordan. The region's monuments tell stories of trade, defense, state administration and encounters between communities.

Castles were never simply battlefields: they were also centres of storage, local governance and daily life. The masonry, gates and hilltop views visible today can help visitors imagine how geography shaped power.

1516–1918

Jordan During the Ottoman Era

For centuries, the territory of present-day Jordan was part of the Ottoman Empire. Local communities lived in towns, farming villages and pastoral areas, connected by trade, pilgrimage and social networks extending across the region.

In the late Ottoman period, the city of As-Salt became a prosperous commercial centre. Its historic buildings, yellow limestone façades, stairways and traditions of shared urban life offer a rich example of Jordan's heritage from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

The Hejaz Railway , constructed in the early twentieth century, linked Damascus with Medina and passed through stations in what is now Jordan. It facilitated travel and later became strategically significant during the First World War and the Great Arab Revolt.

THE BATTLES THAT SHAPED THE REGION
MILITARY HISTORY

Important Battles in Jordan’s History

From early Islamic-era battles to the Great Arab Revolt and modern conflicts, these events help explain the strategic importance of Jordan's valleys, deserts and transport routes.

629 CE

Battle of Mu’tah

Mu’tah · Karak Governorate

One of the earliest encounters between a Muslim force and Byzantine-aligned forces took place near Mu’tah. The battle is closely associated with Zayd ibn Harithah, Ja’far ibn Abi Talib and Abdullah ibn Rawahah. It remains an important site of religious and military memory.

c. 634–635 CE

Battle of Fahl

Pella (Tabaqat Fahl) · Northern Jordan

Fahl, near the ancient city of Pella in the northern Jordan Valley, was the scene of an early confrontation between Muslim and Byzantine forces. Accounts vary over the precise dating, but the battle is connected with the transition from Byzantine to early Islamic rule.

Historical source / tourism reference ↗
636 CE

Battle of Yarmouk

Yarmouk River region · Jordan–Syria borderlands

This decisive battle took place in the wider Yarmouk River region, close to the modern borderlands rather than simply inside present-day Jordan. Its outcome transformed political power in the Levant and helped end Byzantine control over much of the region.

Historical source / tourism reference ↗
1917 CE

The Capture of Aqaba

Aqaba · Southern Jordan

Arab Revolt forces captured Aqaba in July 1917, turning the Red Sea port into an important base for later operations. The event connected the history of today’s Jordan with the wider campaign against Ottoman control during the First World War.

Historical source / tourism reference ↗
1918 CE

Battle of Tafila

Tafila · Southern Jordan

In January 1918, Arab Revolt forces fought Ottoman troops near Tafila. The fighting reflected the importance of the southern highlands, communications routes and the campaign to challenge Ottoman garrisons in the region.

Historical source / tourism reference ↗
21 March 1968

Battle of Karameh

Al-Karameh · Jordan Valley

Jordanian armed forces, alongside Palestinian fighters, fought an Israeli military operation in the Jordan Valley. The battle became an important symbol in Jordan’s national military memory after the 1967 war. Accounts of its wider significance vary, so historical study benefits from consulting more than one perspective.

Historical note

Some ancient battle dates and sites are debated, and historical boundaries differed from those of modern Jordan. The Yarmouk battle is associated with the border region, not a claim that all fighting took place inside modern Jordan.

THE ROAD TO MODERN JORDAN
1916 · THE GREAT ARAB REVOLT

Sharif Hussein bin Ali and the Great Arab Revolt

Sharif Hussein bin Ali , the Hashemite leader based in Mecca, launched the Great Arab Revolt against Ottoman rule in June 1916. His sons, including Abdullah and Faisal, took part in military and political campaigns that transformed the future of the region during and after the First World War.

In the territory of present-day Jordan, operations connected with the revolt included the capture of Aqaba in 1917, attacks on the Hejaz Railway and fighting around Tafila and Ma'an in 1918. The revolt became an important part of the Hashemite political narrative and the story of the modern Jordanian state.

It is important to distinguish the people shown here: Sharif Hussein bin Ali was the leader of the Great Arab Revolt, not one of the four kings of Jordan . His son Abdullah became the founder of the Emirate of Transjordan and later King Abdullah I.

1921–1946

From the Emirate to Independence

Following the First World War and the collapse of Ottoman authority, the region entered a period of major political reorganisation. Abdullah bin Al Hussein arrived in Ma'an in 1920 and moved north to Amman in 1921. The Emirate of Transjordan took shape under his leadership, during a period of British involvement in the territory.

Institutions of government, administration and public life developed in the following decades. The Basic Law of 1928 established an early constitutional framework. On 25 May 1946 , Jordan achieved formal independence and Abdullah became King of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan.

These developments did not erase the region's earlier identities. Rather, they mark the emergence of the modern state whose population drew on the area's urban, rural and Bedouin communities and its long connections across the Levant and Arabia.

The Story and Meaning of Jordan’s Flag

Inspired by the Great Arab Revolt, the Jordanian flag brings together colours and symbols linked to centuries of Arab and Hashemite history. Its present design was formally adopted in 1928.

THE HASHEMITE KINGS OF JORDAN
1921–PRESENT

Four Kings, Four Chapters of Modern Jordan

Meet the four Hashemite monarchs of Jordan. Each reign is presented as a distinct chapter, with the major milestones and historical context that shaped the country.

THE FOUNDING KING · 1921–1951

King Abdullah I bin Al Hussein

Founder of the Emirate of Transjordan and the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan

King Abdullah I was born in Mecca in 1882 and participated in the political and military events surrounding the Great Arab Revolt. Following the war and the collapse of Arab rule in Damascus in 1920, he travelled north and arrived in Ma'an, later reaching Amman.

In 1921, Abdullah became Emir of Transjordan. His government worked to establish institutions of administration, security and political representation in a territory undergoing rapid change. The Basic Law of 1928 and the first legislative elections in 1929 were important developments in this early period.

After independence on 25 May 1946 , he became King of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan. The 1948 Arab–Israeli war and its consequences deeply affected the country during his reign. King Abdullah I was assassinated in Jerusalem on 20 July 1951 .

  • 1921 Foundation of the Emirate of Transjordan
  • 1928–1929 Basic Law and first legislative elections
  • 1946 Jordanian independence and monarchy
  • 1951 End of his reign after his assassination

THE CONSTITUTIONAL KING · 1951–1952

King Talal bin Abdullah

Remembered for the Constitution of 1952

King Talal, the eldest son of King Abdullah I, became monarch in 1951. He had received education in the region and military training at the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst in Britain. His reign was brief, yet it is associated with one of the most consequential institutional documents in Jordan's modern history.

The Constitution of 1952 helped define the constitutional monarchy, the responsibilities of government, the legislative process and the rights and duties of citizens. During his reign, free education also received official recognition, and the state continued developing its legal framework.

Because of illness, Talal could not continue to exercise the duties of the throne. In August 1952, his son Hussein was proclaimed King, while a regency council served until Hussein reached the constitutionally required age to assume his powers.

  • 1951 Accession to the throne
  • 1952 Promulgation of the Constitution of Jordan
  • August 1952 Succession by King Hussein

A REIGN OF TRANSFORMATION · 1952–1999

King Hussein bin Talal

A long reign through major regional change

King Hussein was proclaimed monarch on 11 August 1952 and assumed his constitutional powers on 2 May 1953 . Over nearly five decades, Jordan experienced profound demographic, political and economic changes alongside wars and upheavals across the Middle East.

During his reign, the country expanded education, healthcare and infrastructure while confronting difficult regional pressures. Jordan's role in the 1967 Arab–Israeli war resulted in the loss of control of the West Bank, including East Jerusalem. In 1968 , the Battle of Karameh became a defining event in Jordanian military memory. The domestic conflict of 1970–1971 was another serious chapter in Jordan's modern history.

In the final decades of his rule, Jordan navigated political and economic adjustments, including the resumption of parliamentary elections in 1989. In 1994 , Jordan and Israel signed a peace treaty. King Hussein died on 7 February 1999 , and his son Abdullah II succeeded him.

  • 1953 Assumed constitutional powers
  • 1967–1968 Regional war and Battle of Karameh
  • 1989 Resumption of parliamentary elections
  • 1994 Jordan–Israel peace treaty
  • 1999 End of his reign

1999–PRESENT

King Abdullah II bin Al Hussein

The current monarch of Jordan

King Abdullah II was born in Amman on 30 January 1962 . He studied in Jordan and abroad and completed military education at Sandhurst. Before becoming king, he served in the Jordan Armed Forces, including leadership roles in the country's special forces.

He assumed his constitutional powers as King in February 1999 , following the death of King Hussein. His reign has included initiatives related to economic development, educational opportunities, technology, public-sector administration and Jordan's international relations, alongside ongoing regional and domestic challenges.

In constitutional and political life, the period has seen institutional reforms and debates about representative government, modernisation and the economy. Jordan's cultural heritage — including Petra, As-Salt and other archaeological sites — also remains an important part of its national and international identity.

Because this is a continuing reign, recent policy details and statistics should be checked against up-to-date official publications before they are added to a historical account.

  • 1962 Born in Amman
  • 1999 Acceded to the throne
  • 2009 Prince Al Hussein named Crown Prince
  • Today Continuing reign
